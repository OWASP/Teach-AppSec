
# Part 1: Theory:

## What is risk management:

  * In simple terms risk is the potential of loss as a result of an action or an event.
      * Initial discussion on how this is not achievable in cybersecurity - comparison with established risk disciplines like life insurance and others, where actual data can feed a statistical model of risk - but not on cybersecurity
  * Organisations have threats - external and internal
  * Organisations have assets that have weaknesses / vulnerabilities 
  * Assets have inherent value.
  * Risk is the probability of a threat materialising to exploit a vulnerability resulting in an impact to the organisation.
  * Impact of risk materialisation 
      * = risk * value of asset (from a calculation perspective)
      * = regulatory breach / legal complication / contractual breach / financial impact usually expressed in terms of currency (for example in dollars or pounds or any other local currency)
      * = qualitative risk (in our context this is the real risk, unique risk to the organization, etc.)
  * This is a simplistic representation of risk materialization. There are more
        sophisticated methods  that can help arrive at risk materialisation which include but is not limited to
        OpenFAIR methods (https://www.opengroup.org/open-fair), ALE methods (https://www.fairinstitute.org/blog/fair-beginners-guide-what-do-the-numbers-mean), qualitative risk assessment (https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-30r1.pdf)
      * Discussions of each method, why is OpenFAIR being currently considered the golden measure of risk measurement
  * Examples:
      * A microsoft SQL Server database server has a public IP address, is available on public network and uses username = sa and password = sa
            * Vulnerability = using default credentials which are username = sa and password = sa
            * Threat = a malicious actor on the internet scanning for public IP addresses
            * Risk of threat materialisation = skill of the malicious actor scanning public IP addresses, locating the microsoft SQL Server database and trying “sa” / “sa” on the database.
            * Impact to the organisation = value of the lost data. If this data is personally identifiable information, it could be total number of records and the financial penalty that is applied by the regulatory body
            * Why all this amounts basically to security theater.
  * Because organizations have limited resources (in terms of finance, people, etc), identified risks need to be prioritised such that money can be allocated.
  * To ensure money is allocated to address risks, risks need to be owned by a risk owner who is usually a business function owner like IT, product owner, operations manager, etc.

  * Risks are addressed / treated in four ways:
      * Mitigate 
      * Transfer
      * Avoid
      * Accept
      * This treatment depends on various factors like the availability of resources in house, risk tolerance of the functions / organisation, the extent of damage the organisation can tolerate before they treat the risk (risk tolerance and risk acceptance), etc.
      * Each of the treatments come with a cost:
            * Mitigate: usually involves implementing a process, a procedure or a tool which come with either resource cost like a team or financial cost
            * Transfer: usually involves either outsourcing the activity that produces risk or buying insurance and both cost money
            * Avoid: may lead to designing the delivery of the activity in a different way
            * Accept: is where the risk materialisation may lead to financial loss but the organisation would rather absorb the loss than treat the risk. For example if the organisation suffers a loss of $20,000 with a denial of service attack and the cost of treating this risk is $50,000 and the organisation makes $200,000 yearly, the organisation may choose to absorb $20,000 if and when the denial of service takes place.
  * To ensure that all these activities are addressed in a structured way, it is helpful to create a risk register
      * A risk register usually has the following fields (they may vary slightly or the fields may be named differently depending on the organisation needs and requirements). ## Part 2: Practice:
When you join an organisation:
Week 1:
	Settle in the first week

Week 2:
Talk to the Information security team and understand the problems and issues they have

Make a list of the stakeholders with following roles in departments like IT, product development, architecture, operations management (please note that these departments may be named differently in the organisations you work in):
    * Decision makers  
    * Decision influencers
    * Consulted in decisions
	  This list will come handy later on when mitigations for risks need to be implemented

Week 4:
Ask the head of the information security what keeps them up at night
Check with Information Security team for risk register
If a risk register is available, great use it
If not
    * Work with the product owner, IT team, operations team, (and any other stakeholders that you have identified) to understand what it is that they fear will go wrong from a security perspective
            * Document the fears as threats
    * Work with the same stakeholders to understand what they would like to improve upon 
            * Document those improvements as weaknesses / vulnerabilities
    * With the threats and vulnerabilities, work with the information security team to understand the mitigations
This activity may take anywhere between 4 weeks to several months.

Week “x”:
Prioritisation of risks:

    If the information security team have risk register:
          * You can start on the prioritisation soon after obtaining the list.

    If the risk register has been created by you:
          * It may take some weeks to get to risk prioritisation

    However, irrespective of the the origin of the risk register:
          * Once a risk register is available,
                * Prioritise the risks based on the impact.
                * Confirm the prioritised list with the risk owners 
                * Agree a plan of execution for the risk treatment.
                * Hand over the plan to the project / program management team for implementation of the treatments
                * Review status of the implementation regularly so it can be reported to the governance board (For more details about governance please go through 4. Introduction to Governance, Risk and Compliance).
          * Review all risks on an annual basis

Ongoing activities:
      Risk management has operational / ongoing activities. As a bare minimum, these include:
                * Monitoring of status of risks via regular meetings with risk owners
                * Adding / retiring risks to the risk register
                * Presenting a risk view / heat map to stakeholders.
                * In practice, it also helps in hosting a regular meeting (usually quarterly) with the risk owners so that the latest developments can be discussed.
